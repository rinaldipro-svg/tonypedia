Montreal spent roughly seven billion dollars on a driverless train system that was supposed to be the envy of North America. Instead, it's become a case study in the gap between infrastructure ambition and infrastructure execution — a system riders now approach with a shrug instead of excitement, bracing for the next unexplained delay.

The Réseau express métropolitain, or REM, is the largest public transit project in Quebec in fifty years. It was pitched as transformative: a fully automated light-metro network that would stitch together Montreal's sprawling, car-dependent suburbs with its urban core, unclogging highways and rewriting how hundreds of thousands of people live their daily lives. That vision mattered. Montreal's geography — an island city ringed by bridges and tunnels that turn into parking lots at rush hour — has long needed exactly this kind of structural fix.

But the distance between what REM promised and what REM delivers has become the real story. And it's worth examining closely, because it says something broader about how modern infrastructure projects sell themselves on vision while quietly underdelivering on the unglamorous fundamentals: reliability, redundancy, and basic user experience.

## The Original Promise: Rewiring How a Region Moves

REM's pitch was never just "faster trains." It was a theory of urban change.

Planners framed it as a tool for reshaping commuting patterns across the entire Montreal metropolitan region. The South Shore, the West Island, the airport corridor — areas historically dependent on cars and buses funneling into bottlenecked bridges — would suddenly have direct, frequent, automated rail access to downtown. No drivers, no unions to negotiate frequency with, just computer-controlled trains running every few minutes, aiming for a subway-like cadence of light rail.

The deeper promise was behavioral. Transit planners talk about "mode shift" — the idea that if you make transit fast and reliable enough, people will structurally change how they live. They'll choose to skip buying a second car. They'll choose to move slightly farther out, trusting the train to make the commute painless. They'll stop driving to the airport.

REM was engineered to be the kind of spine that makes those decisions easy. It wasn't just a train — it was meant to be an argument for a different kind of city, one slightly less organized around the automobile.

This is a legitimate and important goal. Cities that successfully pull off this kind of mode shift — Vancouver with its SkyTrain, for instance — see compounding benefits for decades: less highway congestion, more transit-oriented development, denser and more walkable neighborhoods near stations.

## The Reality: A System Defined by Its Failures

Here's the problem: a transit system only changes behavior if people can trust it. And trust is precisely what REM has struggled to build since it began opening in phases starting in 2023.

The complaints aren't abstract. They're specific, recurring, and exactly the kind of thing that erodes commuter confidence fast.

**Unplanned shutdowns have been frequent.** Since launch, the REM has experienced a string of service interruptions — some lasting hours, some affecting entire branches during peak commute windows. For a system explicitly marketed as more reliable than a human-operated one, this is a direct contradiction of the core selling point.

**Communication during disruptions has been poor.** Riders have repeatedly reported vague or delayed updates when something goes wrong, leaving commuters stranded without clear guidance on alternatives. In a driverless system with no conductor to ask, the information architecture — apps, screens, announcements — has to do all the work. When that fails, riders are left genuinely stuck.

**Extreme weather exposes design gaps.** Montreal winters are not a surprise; they're the defining climate reality of the region. Yet the REM has faced weather-related slowdowns and stoppages that suggest insufficient weatherproofing for a system built specifically to serve a cold-climate city. This is the kind of failure that's hard to excuse, because it wasn't unforeseeable — it was foreseeable and apparently under-engineered for.

**The automation trade-off cuts both ways.** Driverless operation was sold as a reliability upgrade — no human error, no labor disputes disrupting service. But full automation also means there's no operator on board to handle anomalies locally. When sensors misfire or doors malfunction, the system can default to a full stop rather than a human judgment call. The thing that was supposed to make REM more dependable has, in some documented cases, become a single point of failure.

**The user experience doesn't match the price tag.** For a system this expensive and this newly built, riders have flagged basic friction points: confusing wayfinding at multi-level stations, inconsistent accessibility experiences, and connections to existing bus and metro networks that feel bolted on rather than integrated. A system designed from scratch had the rare opportunity to get the basics right from day one — and significant portions of its ridership don't feel that happened.

## Why the Gap Matters More Than It Seems

It would be easy to write this off as standard "new infrastructure has growing pains" commentary. But that undersells what's actually at stake.

Mode shift — the entire behavioral premise REM was built on — depends on trust accumulated over time, and trust is asymmetric. It takes years of consistent, boring, reliable service to convince someone to sell their car. It takes one bad stranding during a snowstorm to convince them not to.

Every unplanned shutdown isn't just an inconvenience for that day's riders. It's a data point against the entire thesis the project was funded on. If REM can't convince skeptical suburban commuters that it's more dependable than their car, it will plateau as a nice-to-have rather than becoming the structural alternative it was designed to be. And a seven-billion-dollar structural alternative that doesn't shift structures is a very expensive train.

There's also a quieter institutional cost. REM was developed and is operated by CDPQ Infra, an arm of Quebec's public pension fund manager — a financing model pitched as innovative because it insulated the project from some of the political gridlock that kills transit expansion elsewhere. But that same model means the fund's credibility, not just a transit agency's, is now tied to whether this system can be fixed. Early reliability failures complicate the case for using similar financing structures on the next big infrastructure bet, in Quebec or anywhere else watching this experiment.

## The Takeaway

REM's underlying idea was sound, and arguably still is: automated, frequent, grade-separated rail genuinely can restructure how a metropolitan region moves, as other cities have shown. The ambition was never the problem.

The problem is that Montreal built the vision first and is now retrofitting the reliability — rather than the other way around. Riders don't fall in love with a system's long-term theory of urban change. They fall in love, or out of it, based on whether the train shows up when it's supposed to.

Until REM closes that gap, it will keep being two different stories at once: a genuinely ambitious piece of transit infrastructure on paper, and a daily reminder, for the people actually riding it, of how much harder execution is than vision.
